# Hauler Billing & Pricing: Research Brief for the TrashLab Work Trial

Prepared September 10, 2026. Scope: how small and mid-size US waste haulers price, bill, and collect today, what software they use, and what is public about TrashLab. Figures are examples from real rate pages and contracts, not norms; regional spread is large (West Coast franchise rates run 2 to 3x Midwest and Southern open-market subscription rates). Anything marked **[uncertain]** could not be confirmed from a primary source, and **[varies]** flags practice that differs widely by region or hauler size. Vendor ROI numbers are self-reported unless noted.

One framing note before the sections: the industry has two structurally different pricing regimes. In **franchise markets** (most of California, much of the West, many large cities) the municipality sets a public rate card and the hauler bills against it. In **open or subscription markets** (most of the Midwest, South, Texas, Northeast rural) the hauler sets its own price, competes house by house, and pricing is a trade secret. TrashLab's SMB customers are mostly the second kind; the enterprise and municipal push is the first. A billing product has to handle both.

## 1. How waste haulers price and bill today, by line of business

### Residential (subscription and contract)

**The basic unit is a cart, not a household.** Standard sizes are 35, 64, and 96 gallon (often marketed as 32/65/95). In open markets most small haulers price a flat monthly rate for one 96 gallon tote with weekly pickup, then add per-cart or per-bag extras. In franchise markets the rate is graduated by cart size and often by frequency (weekly vs every-other-week), and multi-cart tiers are on the sheet.

City of Saint Paul (city-billed organized collection) charges a $40 fee if you change cart size more than once per quarter and rolls unpaid balances onto the property tax bill with a 5.95% certification fee ([stpaul.gov](https://www.stpaul.gov/departments/public-works/residential-garbage/garbage-service-and-carts)).

**Add-ons.** Recycling is usually a separate line (biweekly is common in open markets), yard waste is seasonal or per-bag ($4/large yard bag at Preferred; $10/mo or $100.59 for a 10-month season at Waste Connections WA), bulk items are often "2 free per month, then per item." Bundled trash+recycling is the norm in franchise markets and in newer open-market haulers; a la carte is the norm at older Midwest and Southern haulers.

**HOA and municipal contract pricing.** HOA curbside is typically a negotiated per-door rate billed to the association, not the resident; specific per-door curbside rates were not found publicly **[uncertain]**. Valet trash for multifamily is $8 to $17 per unit per month to the property, resold to residents at $25 to $40 ([Impact Trash](https://impacttrash.com/valet-trash-cost-pricing-guide/)). Municipal contracts are either the city paying the hauler a per-household rate (city bills residents on the utility bill) or a franchise where the hauler bills residents directly at the ordinance rate. This distinction matters for billing: in city-billed contracts, the hauler's "customer" is one account with 20,000 service addresses.

**Fuel and environmental surcharges.** The nationals publish the mechanism. Casella's energy fee is a percent of invoice keyed to the EIA/DOE on-highway diesel index (0% at diesel under $2.59; 6.67% at $4.00; 11.27% at $5.00), plus a fixed 9% "environmental" component ([Casella fee table](https://api-prod.casella.com/umbraco/media/rlabcbva/20250901-casella-ee-fee-table-history-tracker.pdf)). Republic charges an 18% Environmental Recovery Fee on hauling charges, a monthly DOE-indexed Fuel Recovery Fee, and a $5.95 admin fee per invoice ([Republic fee disclosures](https://www.republicservices.com/customer-support/fee-disclosures)). WM computes an "energy surcharge" enterprise-wide ([WM T&C](https://www.wm.com/us/en/terms/terms-and-conditions-for-commercial-and-roll-off)). Small haulers split: some copy the model (Trash Taxi's 7% fuel + $1 EPA fee), and many market explicitly against it ("no fuel surcharges" at Home Town, Howie's, Keystone, Trash Daddy). The environmental fee is functionally margin; disposal cost is embedded in the base rate for flat-rate residential.

**Billing frequency.** From roughly a dozen rate pages, **quarterly in advance** looks like the dominant small-hauler residential pattern in the Midwest, South, and Northeast, **monthly in advance** where the hauler pushes autopay, and monthly or bimonthly in franchise areas. Annual prepaid shows up as a discount option (Howie's 11-for-12, Dresnek $262/yr). WM's own residential subscription terms allow monthly, quarterly, or annual in advance with no refund on monthly/quarterly mid-term cancellation and a prorated refund on annual ([WM residential terms](https://www.wm.com/us/en/terms/terms-and-conditions-for-residential-subscription-services)). No survey data exists on the mix **[uncertain, varies]**. Forum reports put open-market prices at $19.50 to $28/mo in the Midwest and PA, $125/quarter in Minneapolis suburbs, $76 to $87 bimonthly for WM in California ([BITOG thread](https://bobistheoilguy.com/forums/threads/garbage-service-how-much-do-you-pay.328238/)).

### Commercial frontload

**Price = f(container size, pickups per week, number of containers) plus a fee stack.** Containers run 1 to 8 yards (2, 4, 6, 8 most common), frequency 1x to 6x per week. The franchise sheets make the matrix explicit: WM SLO 2 yd 1x/wk $228.31/mo up to 5x/wk $1,025.76; 6 yd $432.85 to $2,011.19; a temp 2 to 3 yd bin is $122 to $146 per occurrence. El Dorado prices a per-yard base plus a per-pickup component. Open-market Midwest examples: Milwaukee Dumpster Rental 2 yd $90 to $140, 8 yd $160 to $260/mo at 1x/wk, "all-in per-tip rate" plus a one-time delivery fee, 1 year agreement ([source](https://www.milwaukeedumpsterrental.com/FrontLoadDumpsters)); Keystone Container (PA) 2 yd $90 to $150, 8 yd $200 to $300, "one number covers everything," longer terms $15 to $30/mo cheaper ([source](https://keystonecontainer.com/commercial-dumpster-rental-prices/)). Dumpsters.com cites a $178/mo national average and $20 to $50/mo each for lock bars, wheels, and drain plugs ([source](https://www.dumpsters.com/pricing/commercial-dumpster-prices)).

**How a hauler actually builds the number** (Waste360, [The Art of Commercial Pricing](https://www.waste360.com/commercial-waste/the-art-of-commercial-pricing)): truck at roughly $650/day divided by 80 lifts is about $8.12 per lift; a 4 yd 1x/wk account is roughly $35 truck and labor + $33 disposal (95 lb/yd at $40/ton) + 15% indirect + 15% margin = $88.64/mo. Disposal is about 40% of price and density ranges from 70 lb/yd (office) to 130 lb/yd (restaurant), so the same container costs different amounts to serve depending on what is in it. Haulers deliberately underprice first accounts in a new area to seed route density.

**Per-haul vs flat monthly.** Flat monthly for scheduled frequency is standard; "on-call" or per-haul frontload exists for low-volume accounts. Extra pickups are per-event charges. Overage (lid not closed, bags beside the container) is either a per-yard "excess yardage" charge or a per-incident fee; contamination in recycling containers is a per-incident charge ($20 at El Dorado, WM charges $5 per residential incident). Lock, casters, enclosure, and gate fees are monthly recurring line items or a per-lift "gate fee."

**Contract terms are the most adversarial part of the industry.** WM's commercial and rolloff T&C ([wm.com](https://www.wm.com/us/en/terms/terms-and-conditions-for-commercial-and-roll-off); [Law Insider teardown](https://www.lawinsider.com/resources/contract-teardown/waste-managements-terms-and-conditions)): evergreen auto-renewal; termination notice must land in a window of 90 to 180 days before term end; liquidated damages equal the average of the last six months' charges times six; rate increases allowed for disposal, CPI, fuel, and regulation, plus "consensual price increases" for any other reason deemed accepted unless the customer objects within 30 days. Initial terms run 12 to 60 months, typical annual escalators are around 4%, and 20 to 40% cumulative increases over five years are common ([P3 Cost Analysts](https://www.costanalysts.com/articles/waste-management-contract/), [National Waste Associates](https://www.nationalwaste.com/blog/contract-renewal-traps-automatic-fee-increases-you-missed-in-small-print/)). Right-of-first-refusal clauses show up in DOJ consent decrees against the majors ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/atr/case-document/file/516326/dl)). Small haulers mostly run 1 to 2 year auto-renewing terms with plainer language **[varies]**; whether they use ROFR or liquidated damages is not established **[uncertain]**.

### Rolloff

**Two pricing models coexist, and a billing product must model both.** The "all-in flat rate" model (dominant for homeowners and retail customers): one price for delivery, N days of rental, a tonnage cap, and pickup, with overage billed after the fact. The "haul plus disposal" model (franchise areas and contractor accounts, and how the hauler's own costs actually accrue): a haul fee per pull plus actual tons times a per-ton disposal rate.

Trash Daddy's fee explainer gives the industry ranges: extra days $15 to $35, overage $75 to $100/ton, relocation $100 to $200, dry run $200 to $300, permits $50 to $200 passed through ([source](https://trashdaddydumpsters.com/dumpster-tips/true-cost-hidden-dumpster-fees-overage-fuel-relocation/)). Typical tonnage caps: 10 yd 1 to 2 t, 20 yd 2 to 3 t, 30 yd 3 to 4 t, 40 yd 4 to 5 t ([ZTERS](https://www.zters.com/blog/dumpster-weight-limits-and-overage-fees)). The scale ticket flow is gross minus truck and container tare equals net; the customer is billed only on tons above the cap.

**Material-based pricing.** Mixed C&D or MSW is the default. Clean loads (concrete, dirt, brick, asphalt, clean wood, shingles, yard waste) go to cheaper inert or recycling outlets and are priced separately, usually as a flat "no overweight" box limited to 10 to 15 yards because concrete weighs 3,000 to 4,000 lb per yard and a full 10 yd box can exceed legal road weight ([Chicago Dumpster Pros](https://chicagodumpsterpros.com/blog/concrete-dirt-dumpster-cost/)). A mixed load in a "clean" box gets rejected at the facility and triggers a contamination charge plus re-haul of $200 to $400. Prohibited items (tires, mattresses, appliances with freon, paint) are itemized: WM charges $100 per tire.

**Deposits and prepay.** Residential and retail rolloff is prepaid by card at booking or delivery with the card kept on file for overage and extra days; contractors and commercial accounts run on net 15/30 with monthly bulk invoicing. Docket formalizes this into four client types: Residential/Retail (prepay, per-job invoice), Contractor Prepay, Commercial/Industrial (net terms, bulk bill), Contractor Bulk Bill; prepay invoices are "due on invoice creation" and net-terms charges accumulate "in a Charge Bank until invoicing" ([Docket billing workflow guide](https://support.yourdocket.com/hc/en-us/articles/35745006617997-Docket-Billing-Workflow-Configuration-Guide)). Explicit cash deposits are rare on small-hauler pages; the card authorization substitutes **[uncertain]**.

### Portable toilet, septic, and scale house (brief)

**Portable toilets** are rental billing: a monthly unit rate that includes weekly service ($125 to $275/mo for a standard construction unit), event pricing by day or weekend ($75 to $175/day), extra service visits $30 to $60 each, hand-wash stations $50 to $100/mo, winterization $10 to $20 per cycle, restroom trailers $1,070 to $4,000/wk ([ZTERS](https://www.zters.com/blog/explained-how-much-does-it-cost-to-rent-a-porta-potty)). The billing quirks are unit-level asset tracking (which specific unit is at which site, for how long), rental minimums, and mid-month proration of unit rentals, which ServiceCore reviewers say the leading vendor still does not handle well. Sales tax is often applicable because it is an equipment rental **[varies]**.

**Septic pumping** is job billing: flat by tank size (1,000 gal $300 to $700) or $0.30 to $0.70 per gallon, plus add-ons (locate/dig, filter cleaning, inspection, emergency premium) and sometimes a separate septage disposal fee ([HomeGuide](https://homeguide.com/costs/septic-tank-pumping-cost)). Mostly one-time invoices, paid on site or by card.

**Scale house / transfer station** is point-of-sale ticket billing: per-ton rate by material code (MCMUA NJ $113/ton MSW, $500/ton tires, including a $3/ton state tax and host benefit; Addison County VT $171/ton with a $9 minimum for loads under 100 lb, $1 admin fee per transaction, $5 for weight-only), weigh in / weigh out or stored tare per truck and container, and a split between cash customers (pay at the window, card or check, often "no cash") and account customers (charge to account, billed monthly) ([MCMUA](https://mcmua.com/sw_ts_tippingfees.asp), [Addison County](https://www.addisoncountyrecycles.org/about/resources/fees)). National average tip fee is about $62/ton with a range from $32 (MS) to $124 (AK) ([Dropcurb](https://dropcurb.com/blog/landfill-tipping-fees-by-state)). For a hauler that owns a transfer station, the scale ticket is also the source document for its own rolloff tonnage billing, which is why TrashLab's Scale POS and billing modules are coupled.

### Cross-cutting

**Sales tax [varies widely].** Waste collection is a taxable service in TX, AR, IA (nonresidential), WA (a 3.6% solid waste collection tax rather than general sales tax, applied to collection and disposal fees but not container rental or late fees), and by default in HI, NM, SD, WV ([Avalara](https://www.avalara.com/us/en/learn/whitepapers/service-taxability-by-state.html), [WA DOR](https://dor.wa.gov/book/export/html/1130), [TX Comptroller](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/publications/96-259.php)). Taxability differs by line item (service vs container rental vs fee) and by customer class (residential vs commercial, contractor vs homeowner). Practical implication: tax is a property of the line item and jurisdiction, not the invoice.

**Franchise fees.** Municipal franchise fees are a percent of gross receipts (Jacksonville nonresidential: 17%). Haulers either itemize ("$100 service + $17 franchise fee") or blend. A Jacksonville audit found 6 of 10 haulers excluded admin, late, lease, and fuel fees from "gross receipts" ($1.7M underpaid) and 48% of monthly remittances were late ([Jacksonville Council Auditor #793](https://www.jacksonville.gov/city-council/docs/council-auditor/793-nonresidential-solid-waste-franchise-fees-a.aspx)). Even haulers get this wrong, which is a product opportunity.

**Disposal taxes** are per-ton state or county surcharges embedded in tip fees (PA $6.25/t, IA $4.25/t, NJ $3/t, NC $2/t, San Mateo County $9.89/t) ([ILSR](https://ilsr.org/articles/waste-surcharges/)).

**Minimums.** Transfer station minimums ($9), per-yard minimums for loose trash, minimum overage tonnage charges, and monthly minimums on low-volume commercial accounts.

**Credits and proration [varies, and this is important].** Small haulers overwhelmingly do not prorate or refund mid-cycle. Written policies say "no refunds on the first two months," "we do not deduct for missed pickups," "full monthly fee unless canceled before the next service cycle," and GFL's FAQ states "no proration of billing and you will not be entitled to a refund if service is cancelled during a billing cycle" ([GFL FAQ](https://gflenv.com/frequently-asked-questions/)). WM refunds nothing on monthly/quarterly prepay. The common practice for a mid-cycle upgrade is a next-invoice adjustment or credit memo rather than a true daily prorate; software vendors, TrashLab included, position true proration as a feature. County Waste is the exception that prorates first and last partial months. Missed pickups are usually made up, not credited.

**Disposal pass-through.** Explicit per-ton on franchise rolloff and contractor accounts; embedded in flat rates elsewhere, with "environmental" fees acting as the margin buffer. Disposal cost changes are the most cited justification for rate increases.

**Rate increases.** Franchise: annual CPI-indexed by ordinance. Nationals: annual increases that simply appear on the invoice with a 30-day objection window, plus fee stacking. Small haulers: ad hoc notices citing disposal, fuel, and labor with 2 to 4 weeks' notice (Superior Waste added $0.70/week in April 2025 citing two disposal increases in two years, [source](https://superiorwasteservices.net/residential-price-increase-april-2025/)), or a contractual "5% or CPI-U" after a price-lock term.

**Card fees.** 3% (Preferred), 2.49% (Saint Paul), $1.99 per card payment with ACH free (WM). Surcharge legality varies by state (CT and MA prohibit, others cap around 3 to 4%).

## 2. The billing and payments workflow end to end

**The composite flow at a small hauler office:**

1. **Customer/site record created** (phone call, walk-in, web form, or TrashBolt widget). Address is checked against route coverage by eyeballing a map or by zip; the CSR assigns a route and service day.
2. **Service line added**: container or cart, frequency, material, and rate. The rate comes from a rate card (if the hauler has one), a zone, or whatever the CSR quotes. Setup and delivery fee charged.
3. **Route stop created**; driver gets it on a paper route sheet, a tablet, or the driver app. Cart or container delivered.
4. **Recurring billing** for subscription and frontload happens in advance at the start of the cycle, generated as a batch by "billing cycle" or "payment group." Rolloff retail is charged at booking; rolloff contractor charges accumulate to a monthly bulk invoice.
5. **Exceptions come in from the field**: extra bags, overloaded cart, blocked container (dry run), contamination, extra pickup request. On paper these are notes on the route sheet handed in at end of day; in driver apps they are exception codes with photos. The office turns them into charges, or does not.
6. **Rolloff tonnage** comes back on the scale ticket (paper, emailed PDF, or scale POS); the office computes overage and charges the card on file or adds it to the bulk invoice.
7. **Invoice batch generated** and delivered by print and mail (still common), email, or portal. Nationals charge $5.95 for paper (Republic) or waive the admin charge only with paperless plus autopay (WM).
8. **Payments posted**: check batches keyed by hand or through a lockbox, card by phone, card and ACH through the portal, autopay runs, cash at the scale house or from drivers. Processor deposits arrive as lump sums.
9. **Reconciliation** to QuickBooks: invoices and payments synced or re-keyed; lump-sum processor deposits split against individual invoices; fees booked.
10. **Collections**: statements, late fees at day 5 to 30, suspension after one or two missed cycles, reinstatement fee, cart or container removal, sometimes third-party collections or (in city-billed systems) certification to the property tax roll.

**Service change flow.** "Add a second tote" means: office edits the service line, drivers need to know (delivery work order), and billing needs to know when the new rate starts. At most small haulers the rate simply changes on the next cycle's invoice, and the delivery fee is billed immediately or on the next invoice. "Swap a 4 yd for a 6 yd" on a commercial account is an exchange work order plus a rate change; contracts often allow a price adjustment on size change. The failure modes are the ones vendors write about: the delivery gets done but the rate never changes, or the rate changes but the driver never gets the work order.

**Payment methods.** Check is still material: small haulers publish PO boxes, Hauler Hero pitches batch posting of "cash or check" payments, and Trash Flow argues that recording 100 payments takes 20 minutes in QuickBooks. ACH is pushed as the free option, card carries a convenience fee (or the hauler eats 3%). Autopay is incentivized ($1/mo off) or mandatory on price-lock contracts. Docket lets drivers take payment at the job site. No hard autopay adoption stats exist **[uncertain]**; vendor claims of 30 to 50% faster collections are marketing.

**Collections, late fees, suspension.** Flat $7 to $20 late fees or 1.5%/month finance charges; a two-pickup grace period; suspension; reinstatement fees of $5 to $25; a "reopen" fee after 60 days closed; cart removal charged at about one month's service. WM's residential terms cap late fees at a $5 minimum and Republic at the greater of $5 or 10%. A nice operational detail: Home Town requires a suspended account to pay 24 hours before pickup day or lose the week, because the route is already built.

**Reconciliation pain, documented.** Docket's help center explains that Payrix and Stripe "deposit multiple customer payments as one lump sum," so staff must group payments into Undeposited Funds, add processor fees as negative lines, and match to the bank feed or revenue gets duplicated ([Docket QBO reconciliation](https://support.yourdocket.com/hc/en-us/articles/39982359181197-Reconcile-Batch-Payments-in-QuickBooks-Online-QBO)). ServiceCore's one-way QuickBooks sync "dumps everything into the parent account" per Capterra reviewers. Routeware names "the extra pickup that never gets charged" and "uncharged extras, unnecessary credits, and hours of manual reconciliation" as the core leakage ([Routeware](https://routeware.com/blog/waste-billing-software/)). Hauler Hero's overload article describes drivers logging exceptions with photos so the office can "follow up, educate, or escalate," and notably stops short of auto-charging ([Hauler Hero blog](https://blog.haulerhero.com/residential-cart-overloads-the-hidden-margin-killer-on-every-route-and-how-haulers-get-ahead-of-it)), which suggests most haulers still convert exceptions to charges by hand, if at all.

**The most painful and error-prone steps, ranked by how often they show up in vendor docs, reviews, and hauler policies:**

1. Converting driver-reported exceptions (extra bags, overloads, dry runs, contamination) into billed charges. This is the biggest leakage and the most disputed line on the invoice, so haulers often skip it rather than fight.
2. Rolloff overage after the scale ticket arrives, especially against a prepaid card that may decline or a contractor who disputes the tare.
3. Mid-cycle service changes on advance-billed accounts: credits, next-invoice adjustments, rate effective dates, and the cart delivery that happens before the rate change lands.
4. Lump-sum processor deposits vs per-invoice payments in QuickBooks, plus one-way sync duplication.
5. Applying the right tax and franchise fee per line item and customer class; even haulers misreport franchise fee bases.
6. Annual price increases across thousands of accounts, contract renewal windows, and escalator tracking; small haulers do this with a mail merge.
7. Manual check posting, misapplied payments, and lockbox exceptions.
8. Multi-site commercial customers who want one invoice with per-site detail, or per-site invoices with one statement.

## 3. The data model in practice

Vendor help docs (Docket, CurbWaste, Hauler Hero, ServiceCore, Trash Flow, TrashLab) converge on the same hierarchy, and franchise rate sheets confirm it from the pricing side:

**Customer (billing entity)** owns terms and billing behavior: client type (Docket: Residential/Retail, Contractor Prepay, Commercial/Industrial, Contractor Bulk Bill), payment terms (prepay vs net 30), billing cycle or "payment group," delivery method (mail, email, portal), tax status, autopay method on file, contract and escalator. TrashLab's own billing page speaks of "contract rates and price escalators" at this level.

**Site / service address / location** is the operational unit: route, service day, gate codes, access notes, container assets on site. Docket also models dump locations and yard locations as first-class places. In franchise commercial pricing the rate is keyed per container per site.

**Service** = container (size, type, or a specific serialized asset) + frequency + material or waste stream + rate. Recurring services generate route stops and advance charges; one-time jobs (delivery, haul, swap, extra pickup, dry run) generate event charges. TrashLab lists billable events as "completed jobs, route events, container movement (drops, swaps, pickups), and disposal data."

**Price** attaches at three levels that must resolve in a defined order: the rate card or zone (TrashBolt polygons; Docket location-based rates by zip and customer type; ServiceCore service-area pricing tiers), the contract override on the account, and the line-level surcharges and taxes (fuel %, environmental %, franchise %, sales tax by jurisdiction and line type).

**Charges → Invoice → Payment**, with credit memos, voids, refunds, merged invoices, a billing queue, and an exceptions or "needs action" tab (CurbWaste knowledge base categories).

**Where it gets messy:**

- **One customer, many sites.** A property manager with 40 buildings, a restaurant group, a city with 20,000 households, or a contractor with 15 active rolloffs. They want one invoice grouped by site, or one invoice per site with one statement, or per-site invoices sent to different AP contacts. Parent/child accounts are a documented gap even at Hauler Hero ("work in progress" per reviewers) and the core reason haulers say QuickBooks alone "requires workarounds for multi-location customer accounts."
- **One site, many lines of business.** A construction site with a frontload container, three rolloffs, and four portable toilets, each billed on a different cadence (monthly flat, per pull plus tons, monthly rental plus per-service). The site is one thing to the customer and three things to the hauler's routes and rate cards.
- **One-time vs recurring on the same account.** A residential subscriber orders a rolloff for a remodel; a frontload account requests an extra pickup. Does the one-time charge ride on the next quarterly invoice or get its own invoice and prepay? Small haulers do both, inconsistently.
- **Contract vs rate card.** Every commercial account is negotiated, so the rate card is a starting point and the "real" price lives in the contract line, with its own escalator and effective dates. When the hauler raises the rate card, contract accounts should not move; when the escalator fires, only that account moves. Systems that store one price per service cannot express this.
- **Asset vs service.** Rolloff and portable toilets bill against a specific serialized box or unit (which one, where, since when); residential and frontload bill against a service definition (a 96 gal cart, any 96 gal cart). Mixing the two models in one schema is where rental-day billing and inventory tracking break.
- **Effective dating.** Rate changes, service starts and stops, holds, and escalators all need effective dates, and advance billing means a change today affects an invoice already sent. Most small-hauler policy ("no proration") is a workaround for not having effective-dated pricing.
- **Tax and fees as line properties.** Washington taxes collection but not container rental or late fees; franchise fees apply to some line types; contractor rolloff is taxable in Texas while residential new construction is exempt. The line item needs a type and the customer needs a class.

### Haulers without dedicated software

There are roughly 21,000 US waste collection businesses **[rough, unattributed]**; Hauler Hero (~120 customers at seed), CurbWaste (~150), CRO (~300), and Docket/ServiceCore (~1,500) together cover a small fraction, so most small haulers run on legacy desktop tools or generic software.

What they actually use: QuickBooks (Desktop is still common at established haulers; Basestation reviewers explicitly ask for Enterprise/Desktop sync) with recurring invoices plus spreadsheets plus handwritten route sheets. ServiceCore's SERC case study describes a portable toilet and dumpster operator with thousands of customers running on "handwritten notes, error-prone spreadsheets, and QuickBooks" at 25 to 30 hours a week of manual job management ([source](https://servicecore.com/blog/spreadsheets-to-software/)). Waste360 quotes a consultant saying "a large portion of the industry still uses physical maps and pins" ([source](https://www.waste360.com/route-optimization/haulers-beginning-to-embrace-route-optimization-tools)). Rolloff operators try Jobber or Housecall Pro first because they already run junk removal on it, then hit the wall on rental-window tracking, tonnage reconciliation, and net-terms invoicing above about three trucks ([OptimizeIt](https://optimizeit.ai/resources/blog/dumpster-rental-software-vs-jobber-vs-housecall-pro)). Some sell quarterly service as a WooCommerce product (Dresnek). Square and Stripe invoicing show up at the one-truck level.

Why they stay: no license fee and spreadsheets bend to exceptions; the dispatcher is the routing engine and the sheet is the notation; migration of thousands of accounts with balances and card tokens is the scariest project the office will ever run; a one-time license (Trash Flow) beats a subscription for owners who think in equipment terms; per-truck pricing punishes growth; contract horror stories (Hauler Hero enforcing fees after a business sale, DRS's new 1% fee for not routing payments through it); and adoption tracks generational handoff in management. Trash Flow's own comparison page concedes QuickBooks' real limits: no routing, no container tracking, no automatic surcharging, and 100 payments take 20 minutes to post.

**The best portals today** (Republic's is the most complete public feature list, [source](https://www.republicservices.com/customer-support/online-account-updates)): pay with saved methods, one-click autopay, paperless toggle, pending payment status, invoice history, schedule an extra or bulk pickup, report a missed pickup, repair or replace a container, relocate a container, gate and lock access details, seasonal stop and restart, multi-site switching for commercial, Track My Truck ETA. On the vendor side, Hauler Hero offers pay, autopay, history, upcoming service, and pickup requests; TrashLab's portal page adds structured service requests that become dispatchable work, rolloff quote requests with service-area and capacity checks, and multi-contact commercial accounts ([trashlab.com portal page](https://trashlab.com/waste-customer-portal-software)); Docket, CurbWaste, DRS, and Bin Boss all offer online dumpster booking with real-time inventory and card on file for overages.

**The nudges that work at the nationals:** WM waives its per-invoice admin charge only for paperless plus autopay; ACH free, card $1.99. Republic charges $5.95 per paper invoice. The nationals' Trustpilot scores (WM 2.6/5 across 2,000+ reviews) are driven by surprise fee increases and stop-service fees, which is exactly the opening small haulers market into.

**How new residential customers are typically onboarded.** Address entered (phone, form, or widget) → service-area check (zip level at most haulers; parcel-level polygons with TrashBolt, which rejects the wrong side of a street or a competitor's HOA) → choose cart size and add-ons → pick a start date → capture payment, usually a full first cycle in advance plus a setup or delivery fee ($27.95 at Trash Taxi, up to $75 at Republic) → cart delivered before first pickup → first invoice. Whether the first partial cycle is prorated is inconsistent: WM and GFL do not refund mid-cycle, County Waste prorates, small haulers mostly bill a full cycle **[uncertain]**. Small hauler examples: Trash Taxi (GA) full online signup via TrashBolt; Red Oak Sanitation (GA) online with price "based on number of cans" and payment up front; Frontier Waste (TX) form then a rep calls; Texas Pride Disposal "request a proposal" with a 3-month non-refundable commitment; Florida Express "check availability and pricing" gated.

**TrashBolt is the key data point on conversion.** Built by the founders of GarbageMan Inc. after selling to WM, it reports that haulers see about 50% of new signups arrive through the portal, 30% of those after hours, and that "if prospects must leave messages, sales drop by 50%" ([Waste360 profile](https://www.waste360.com/fleet-technology/successful-hauler-sells-to-wm-now-helps-haulers-grow)). Docket's only quantified booking stat is "2 to 3 jobs per month haulers weren't getting before," and that only about 10% of cart abandoners call instead ([source](https://www.yourdocket.com/why-this-hauler-switched-to-docket-dumpster-software/)).

**What stops haulers from publishing prices online:**

1. Route density economics: the same house costs $8 or $25 to serve depending on whether the truck is already on the street, so haulers price by street and deliberately underprice to seed new areas.
2. Address-dependent serviceability: zip-level pricing accepts addresses the route cannot serve; the fix (polygons) is real work most haulers have not done.
3. Franchise agreements set the price by ordinance, so there is nothing to publish, and nationals exclude franchise areas from promos.
4. Surcharge stacking makes a single headline price misleading; publishing "$29/mo" invites the "plus 18%" fight.
5. Competitor visibility and anchoring: Bin Boss ships a "competitor rate scanner by zip," which tells you published prices get scraped. **[inferred, no hauler quoted saying this]**
6. Commercial pricing is negotiated per account by waste density, access, and timing; there is no rate card to publish.
7. Disposal cost variance (tip fees from $35/ton in Cleveland to $160 in Boston) makes rolloff pricing inherently local.

## 7. Open questions for user discovery

Questions marked ★ are the ones most likely to surface a product tradeoff you will have to decide.

### Residential

1. ★ Walk me through what happens between a new customer calling and their first invoice. Who touches it, and where does the price come from? (Surfaces rate card vs CSR discretion vs zone pricing, and whether address-based pricing is even possible for them.)
2. ★ When someone cancels or upgrades in the middle of a quarter, what do you actually do with the money? (Surfaces proration policy vs capability; most will say "we don't prorate," and you need to know if that is policy or a system limitation.)
3. What percent of residential is quarterly in advance vs monthly, and what percent is on autopay? What would it take to move more onto autopay? (Sizes the collections problem and the card-fee tradeoff.)
4. Do you charge a fuel or environmental surcharge, and how do you set it? If not, is that a marketing choice against the nationals? (Surfaces whether surcharge logic needs to be a first-class primitive or a line item.)
5. When a driver tags an overloaded cart or extra bags, how often does that become a charge, and who decides? (Surfaces the exception-to-charge pipeline and the dispute tolerance.)
6. How do HOA and municipal contract accounts get billed differently from subscribers, and how many households sit behind one bill? (Surfaces the parent/child and bulk-account model.)

### Commercial frontload

7. ★ Show me one commercial contract. Where does that price live in your system today, and what happens when your rate card goes up 5%? (Surfaces contract override vs rate card, escalators, effective dating.)

## The 5 to 7 things most likely to surprise someone new to this industry

1. **Most small haulers do not prorate, on purpose.** "No refunds," "we do not deduct for missed pickups," and "full cycle unless canceled before the next cycle" are written policy, partly because the systems cannot effective-date a rate and partly because the money is small and the arguments are not. A product that makes proration easy will be adopted by some haulers and deliberately ignored by others.

2. **The invoice is built from the truck, not the office.** The billable events (extra bags, overloaded cart, dry run, tonnage, container swap) originate with a driver or a scale, and the biggest revenue leak in the industry is the exception that never becomes a charge. That is why TrashLab's pitch is "proof of service attached before send" rather than "faster invoicing."

3. **Franchise and open markets are different businesses.** In franchise areas the city publishes the rate sheet, the hauler cannot negotiate, and fees like $65.02 redelivery are set by ordinance. In open markets the price is a trade secret set by route density, and the same house can cost $8 or $25 to serve depending on whether the truck is already on the street.

4. **The "environmental fee" is margin, and small haulers compete by not charging it.** Republic's 18% environmental recovery fee, Casella's 9% plus diesel-indexed energy fee, and $5.95 paper invoice fees are the nationals' model; "no fuel surcharges, no admin fees" is the small hauler's marketing line. Surcharge logic has to be first-class for some customers and invisible for others.

5. **Quarterly in advance and paper checks are still normal.** A meaningful share of residential accounts are billed 90 days ahead, paid by check to a PO box, and posted by hand, and the QuickBooks lump-sum deposit problem is the single most common billing complaint across every vendor's reviews.

6. **Commercial contracts are adversarial by design.** Evergreen auto-renewal, 90-to-180-day notice windows, liquidated damages of six months' charges, and "consensual" price increases deemed accepted after 30 days are standard at the majors and the subject of DOJ consent decrees. Renewal tracking and escalators are a billing feature, not a CRM feature.

7. **Nobody in the market natively does address-priced online signup, and the one company that does (TrashBolt) was built by haulers who sold to WM.** Their number is that about half of new residential signups arrive online, 30% after hours, when a hauler offers it. Every major platform, TrashLab included, bolts it on.
